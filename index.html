Revisiting Our Risk Assessment Form: An Auditor’s Perspective on Asset Ownership and Control Effectiveness

Risk Assessment  •  Reflection

A Risk Assessment Form is more than a document used to identify cybersecurity threats. It is a record of how an organization understands its assets, evaluates potential risks, and decides whether the protections it claims to have are actually working.

When my group first completed our Risk Assessment Form for a small organization, we were focused on listing threats and recommending treatments. Looking back at it as an auditor, I am asking different questions: Does this organization really know what it owns? Does every asset have a responsible person? And if someone demanded proof that the controls work, could the organization produce it?

Starting with the inventory problem

An auditor’s first question is almost always, “What assets are in scope?” You cannot protect, classify, or assign ownership to something you have not identified. NIST’s guide to risk assessments treats asset identification as foundational because threats, vulnerabilities, and impact can only be judged in relation to specific assets (Joint Task Force Transformation Initiative, 2012). In our assessment, we identified our key assets, including customer records, core business systems, employee devices, and cloud accounts. Honestly, our list was uneven. Some assets were described in detail, while others were grouped loosely, and an auditor would likely flag that grouping as a weakness.

Which assets mattered most

The assets that mattered most were those where a compromise would cause the greatest harm to confidentiality, integrity, or availability. For our organization, these were customer personal data, financial records, and the systems that process transactions. These deserved the highest priority because a compromise would create legal exposure, interrupt operations, and damage trust.

Classification and ownership

Each important asset should carry a classification label, such as public, internal, confidential, or restricted, based on how damaging disclosure or loss would be. Classification then drives handling rules: who may access the asset, how it is stored, and how it is disposed of. ISO/IEC 27001 expects organizations to maintain an inventory of assets, assign owners, and classify information, which makes ownership a formal requirement rather than a courtesy (International Organization for Standardization, 2022).

Ownership is where many organizations quietly fail. An owner is not simply the person who uses an asset or the IT staff who maintain it. The owner is the person accountable for deciding how the asset is protected and who can access it. Looking at our form, I would classify our assets this way: customer data as Confidential and owned by an operations role, the public website as Public and owned by a marketing role, and financial records as Restricted and owned by a finance role. When reviewing our original form, I asked whether ownership was documented clearly enough to survive an audit. An auditor would want named roles, not vague assumptions, and I am deliberately describing roles here rather than individuals to protect the organization’s confidentiality.

What evidence an audit would require

This is the part that changes how you think about controls. An auditor does not accept “we have a policy” or “we do backups” as proof. They look for evidence, meaning records that show a control exists, is operating, and is consistent over time. For the controls our group recommended, an audit would reasonably ask for:

  • Access control: user access lists, records of access reviews, and logs showing removal of accounts when people leave.
  • Backups: backup schedules, completion logs, and documented restoration tests showing data can actually be recovered.
  • Awareness training: attendance records, training materials, and results of any phishing simulations.
  • Patching and updates: patch logs or reports showing systems are kept current.
  • Policies: approved, dated policy documents with evidence that staff have read and acknowledged them.

NIST’s control catalog reinforces this idea by describing controls in terms of how they are implemented, assessed, and monitored, not merely whether they are written down (Joint Task Force, 2020). A control that exists only on paper is, from an auditor’s perspective, close to having no control at all.

Gaps an auditor would likely uncover

Reviewing our form critically, I would expect an auditor to raise several findings in general terms:

  1. Incomplete asset visibility. Some assets, especially personal devices, third-party cloud services, and unofficial tools, may not be captured at all.
  2. Informal ownership. Responsibility may be understood verbally but not documented.
  3. Untested controls. Controls like backups and incident response may be assumed to work without ever being tested.
  4. Missing review cycles. Risk assessments and access rights need regular scheduled review, and an auditor would look for a documented schedule.
  5. Thin evidence trail. Even where good practices exist, records proving them are often scattered or absent.

None of these gaps means the organization is careless. They are common in small and mid-sized organizations where security responsibilities are shared informally. But an auditor reads the evidence, not the intentions.

How an asset inventory and audit checklist would strengthen our treatments

If I could revise our recommendations, I would begin with two simple tools.

First, a centralized asset inventory listing each asset, its type, location, classification, owner, and review date. This makes ownership explicit, reveals forgotten assets, and lets risk treatments be tied to specific items. Instead of recommending “improve access control” in general, we could say “restrict access to the customer records system and review its access list quarterly, owned by the operations manager role.”

Second, an audit checklist that turns each treatment into a verifiable question: Is the control documented? Who is responsible? What evidence proves it works? When was it last tested? With a checklist, a treatment stops being a suggestion and becomes something that can be measured and demonstrated. Together, these tools close the loop between identifying a risk, treating it, and proving the treatment works.

Final reflection

Revisiting our Risk Assessment Form through an auditor’s eyes taught me that good risk management depends as much on accountability and evidence as on technical safeguards. Knowing what you own, assigning responsibility for it, and being able to prove your controls work are what separate a document that looks thorough from a security program that is actually dependable.
